# What a fixed connectome can and cannot do: evolutionary search over command-interneuron stimulation in a whole-body *C. elegans* model

## Abstract

The *C. elegans* connectome is complete, yet which behaviours its wiring can produce, and which it cannot, remains an open question. We asked this question in a whole-body simulation in which the only free element is a learning agent. The agent may inject current into a short whitelist of interneurons, never into motor neurons or muscles; the 302-neuron OpenWorm c302 network with fitted dynamics, a literature-based motor layer and a viscoelastic body on agar do the rest. Every hypothesis and its verdict were fixed before training. Stimulating five command and steering interneurons (AVB, AVA, SMDD, SMDV, RIV) was sufficient for chemotaxis to a source 2.5 mm away in 91 % of trials, whereas stimulating eight sensory neurons was no better than chance, reproducing at the behavioural level a silence of the sensory-to-command step found earlier in the fitted network. Evolution rediscovered the pirouette rule, reversing and executing a deep bend when concentration fell, and ablations showed that reversal, deep bend and steering were all necessary. The wiring itself mattered: shuffling the synaptic targets while preserving weights, counts and signs cut performance to a fifth, and this loss was carried entirely by the gap-junction layer. In corridors four body widths wide, corners were passed only by a deep bend pressed against the wall, so the dorsal or ventral direction of the bend fixed the direction of the turn; with ventral bends only, right turns were impossible and the worm always entered the ventral arm of a T-maze. Because temporal sensing cannot distinguish the arms at a junction, the learned strategy was to enter one arm and reverse when the odour faded; a left–right concentration difference was needed before the bend direction followed the source. The model yields three testable predictions for narrow-maze assays, and it delimits what the connectome contributes to navigation from what must be supplied by sensing and by the body.

## Author summary

Knowing every connection in a nervous system does not tell us what the wiring does. We took the complete wiring diagram of the worm *C. elegans*, kept every connection fixed, and let a learning algorithm discover what it could achieve by stimulating a handful of neurons, in the same way an experimenter with optogenetic tools could. The algorithm was forbidden to touch the neurons that drive muscles. Through the wiring alone it learned to find an odour source, and it did so by rediscovering the strategy real worms use: reverse and turn sharply when the smell gets weaker. It failed when given only sensory neurons, telling us that the sensory-to-command step is not captured by the fitted wiring. Randomising the wiring destroyed the behaviour, and only the electrical (gap-junction) connections were needed. In narrow mazes, the worm could only turn in the direction of its sharp bend, so a worm that bends ventrally always turns left and needs a costly reversal to reach food on the right. These results separate what the connectome contributes from what the body and the senses contribute, and they make predictions that can be tested with worms in mazes.

## Introduction

The wiring diagram of *C. elegans* has been complete for decades [4,5], but a wiring diagram is not a functional model, and the electrophysiological parameters that would turn one into the other are largely unknown [1,6]. Two responses to this gap dominate. One fits network dynamics to measured signal propagation, as in the recent atlas of Randi et al., which found that the anatomical connectome predicts only part of the functional map [2]. The other fills the unknown parameters by optimisation against behaviour, generating an ensemble of models consistent with anatomy [1]. Both approaches ask what the wiring *does*. Here we ask a complementary question: with the wiring fixed and the free parameters confined to a learning agent outside the nervous system, which behaviours can be produced through the connectome, and which cannot?

We built a whole-body simulator in which the OpenWorm c302 network [6] is coupled to the neuromechanical motor layer of Boyle, Berri and Cohen [7] and to a two-dimensional viscoelastic body crawling on agar, with walls. The network dynamics were fitted once to the signal-propagation atlas [2] and then frozen. A small policy, trained by evolution strategies [8], observes the odour at the nose and may inject current into a whitelist of sensory, command and steering interneurons. It may never stimulate motor neurons or muscles. Behaviour therefore emerges only from what the stimulated interneurons can do through the fixed wiring, exactly the situation of an optogenetic experiment on a circuit whose downstream connectivity cannot be changed.

This framing turns the connectome into an experimental subject. Because the agent, not the model, adapts, a failure to learn a behaviour is evidence about the wiring rather than about our choice of parameters, and a success tells us which interneurons are sufficient. It also lets us compare the learned strategy with the strategies of real animals. Chemotaxis in *C. elegans* is produced by pirouettes, sharp reorientations whose probability rises when the concentration falls [9], and by a weathervane mechanism that steers gradually toward higher concentration [10]. Sharp reorientations are executed by a stereotyped sequence in which SMD and RIV drive the omega bend and SMDD versus SMDV set its dorsal or ventral direction, which the animal chooses according to the odour gradient [11]. Real animals in narrow T-mazes show no innate side bias and find a food arm in 85 % of trials, a behaviour that requires mechanosensation [3]. These quantitative anchors let us judge whether an agent constrained by the connectome converges on the same solutions.

We pre-registered each hypothesis with its judgement criterion before training and kept every negative result. We report that five command and steering interneurons are sufficient for chemotaxis while sensory neurons are not; that evolution rediscovers the pirouette rule and that reversal, deep bend and steering are all required; that the real wiring outperforms shuffled wiring through its gap-junction layer; and that in narrow corridors the direction of the deep bend is the direction of the turn, which makes arm choice at a T-junction a problem of bend direction and lateral sensing rather than of the connectome.

## Results

### The model and the whitelist principle

The agent's only actions are stimulation amplitudes for whitelisted neurons (Fig 1). Every 0.5 s a multilayer perceptron with 16 hidden units maps the recent history of log odour concentration at the nose, and in maze tasks the wall contact of the head, to currents between 0 and 6 pA injected into the chosen neurons of the c302 C2 network (302 neurons, 3,364 neuron-to-neuron chemical synapses, 1,084 gap junctions) with the fitted dynamics "Vfit". Membrane potentials of AVB, AVA, SMDD, SMDV and RIV are read out by the motor layer: the forward and backward gates of the bistable motor units open when the AVB or AVA depolarisation exceeds 3 mV [7]; the SMDD − SMDV difference biases the twelve anterior body rows; and a RIV depolarisation above 30 mV fires a travelling deep-bend pulse that produces an omega turn [11]. Following the neural sequence described for directed turning [11], the dorsal or ventral direction of the pulse is set by the sign of SMDD − SMDV (rule ADR-017); the earlier ventral-only rule (ADR-016) is retained as a control. Policies were trained with OpenAI evolution strategies on a GPU batch simulator with a near-source curriculum (1.5 mm, then 2.5 mm) and evaluated on 128–256 fresh random starts under the pre-registered protocol.

### Five command and steering interneurons suffice for chemotaxis; sensory neurons do not

Stimulating only AVB, AVA, SMDD, SMDV and RIV was sufficient for chemotaxis (Fig 2A–B). After the curriculum, the policy reached a source 2.5 mm away within 40 s in 91.4 % of 256 trials (Wilson 95 % CI 0.873–0.943; median 24 s), against 4.3 % for a random policy and 0 % for a still worm. Across three independent training seeds the reach rates were 0.914, 0.973 and 0.301 (mean 0.73 ± 0.37, s.d.); the third seed converged to a reversal-only strategy that never used the deep bend and stayed there after 100 further generations (0.324), a local optimum we return to below. *[E6c: two more seeds are training; the sentence will be updated to five seeds.]* The whitelist interface cost nothing in reach rate: an oracle controller that knew the source direction and steered the same body directly reached the source in 89.5 % of trials, fewer than the learned policy, although it was faster (median 16.5 s versus 24 s). Learning failed without three ingredients that we record as negative results: a living cost and a terminal distance penalty (otherwise standing still is a local optimum), relative rather than absolute concentration observations, and the curriculum (direct training at 2.5 mm reached 8 % after 45 generations).

Stimulating sensory neurons instead of interneurons did not produce chemotaxis. With the eight neurons ASE, AWC, AWA, ASK, ASH, PLM, ALM and AVM as the only channels, the best policy reached a source 1.5 mm away in 10.9 % of trials (CI 0.077–0.154) against 12.5 % for a random policy (CI 0.090–0.171), and the learning curve stayed at the reward of a still worm for 80 generations. The policy had tried to exploit a sub-millivolt asymmetry of the touch circuit, with the sign opposite to the biological one, consistent with our earlier finding that in the fitted network static sensory stimulation does not select a command state and that 83 % of inhibitory responses in the atlas cannot be explained by chemical synaptic paths (Methods). The difference between the two conditions, 0.996 versus 0.109 at 1.5 mm, is the contribution of a single missing step, sensory-to-command selection.

### Evolution rediscovers the pirouette rule, and reversal, deep bend and steering are all necessary

The learned policy implemented the pirouette rule of Pierce-Shimomura et al. [9] (Fig 2C–D). Sweeping the policy over the observed concentration change gave a clean switch: when concentration fell, AVA (reverse) was driven at 0.98 of maximum with a ventral head bias (SMDV 0.83); when it rose, AVB (forward), RIV (deep bend) and SMDD were driven at 0.89, 1.0 and 0.99. The probability of a reversal onset fell monotonically across quintiles of dC/dt, from 0.53 to 0.06 per second (ratio 9.5, Spearman ρ −0.90), the sign and monotonicity reported for real animals, whose baseline rate is 0.023–0.030 per second; the model's baseline (0.23 per second) is 2–8 times higher because its reversal bouts are short (2 s) and frequent. Restricting the channels to AVB, AVA and RIV, the condition of the pre-registered rule test, produced the rule in its purest form, reverse plus deep bend on decline and forward run on rise (ρ −1.00), in two of three seeds, yet these policies could not navigate (reach 0.05–0.11 at 2.5 mm). Without steering the only reorientation available is a fixed bend of about 180°, so the worm retraces its path. The third seed found an alternative, a bend held continuously during runs, that reached 0.328.

All three components were necessary (Fig 3). Masking one channel of the trained policy at evaluation reduced the reach rate from 0.914 to 0.098 (AVA), 0.066 (RIV), 0.273 (SMDD and SMDV together), 0.434 (AVB) and 0.410 or 0.621 (SMDD or SMDV alone). Retraining without RIV reached 0.191 and retraining without steering 0.05, so the necessity is not an artefact of the particular policy. Our pre-registered prediction that the deep bend would be dispensable on open ground was therefore false: with temporal sensing only, klinokinesis requires a large reorientation. The sleep-active neuron RIS was never recruited. When the task rewarded dwelling at the source, with or without a movement cost, the policy stopped by withdrawing command drive below the gate threshold, and RIS stimulation stayed at zero; RIS can only matter when the forward drive is not under the agent's control.

### The real wiring outperforms shuffled wiring, through its gap junctions

The wiring contributed even though the command neurons were stimulated directly (Fig 6B). We shuffled the postsynaptic targets of all 3,364 neuron-to-neuron chemical synapses and one end of all 1,084 gap junctions, preserving every weight, count and sign, and retrained under the same curriculum and rules. Three shuffled networks reached 0.141, 0.145 and 0.320 (mean 0.202) against 0.871 for the real wiring under the same rule set, and the real-wiring policy transferred to the shuffled networks reached 0.023–0.055. The deficit tracked the deep bend: in 128 evaluation episodes the real network fired 6,960 pulses, the three shuffled networks 0, 670 and 3,599, in the same order as their reach rates. Direct stimulation raises the potential of the stimulated neuron regardless of wiring, so the wiring's contribution lies in how the stimulus spreads to the readout neurons.

That contribution was carried by the gap-junction layer alone. Shuffling only the chemical synapses left performance intact (retrained 0.859, transferred policy 0.914, 6,493 pulses), whereas shuffling only the gap junctions collapsed it (0.078 and 0.125, 116 pulses); the confidence intervals of the two layer-specific controls do not overlap (Table 2). This double dissociation must be read with the fitted dynamics in mind: the fit to the atlas scaled excitatory chemical conductances by 0.032 and inhibitory ones by 0.002 while leaving gap junctions at 1.14, so in this model the neuron-to-neuron chemical layer is nearly silent by construction. The statement we can defend is that, in the atlas-fitted dynamics, the stimulus reaches SMD and RIV through the gap-junction network, in line with the atlas itself, in which gap junctions and non-synaptic signalling account for much of functional connectivity [2].

### In narrow corridors the direction of the deep bend is the direction of the turn

Walls changed the repertoire (Fig 4). We added a wall potential to the body model and lattice mazes with corridors 0.3 mm wide, about four body widths, the ratio used in microfluidic assays. In corridors of this width the omega bend, whose free diameter is 0.34 mm, is blocked (reorientation +2° instead of +170°), forward speed halves (0.07–0.10 versus 0.15–0.18 mm/s), and the odour policy trained on open ground, placed in an L-shaped corridor without further training, passed the corner in 100 % of 128 episodes.

Corners were taken by one mechanism only. Frame-by-frame traces showed the sequence stall → reversal → forward run with a deep-bend pulse pressed against the wall, after which the body slides around the corner; in 64 of 64 corner passages by the open-ground policy, and 64 of 64 by a maze-trained policy, a reversal command and a bend pulse both occurred within the 3 s before passage. Head steering alone could not turn the corner: constant SMDD or SMDV bias with forward drive passed 0 of 32 corners, and masking RIV reduced T-maze completion to 0. The wall converts a blocked omega turn into a slide along the open side, so the dorsal or ventral direction of the bend is the direction of the turn. Under the ventral-only rule, policies passed left-turn corridors in 32 of 32 trials and right-turn corridors in 0 of 32, at corridor widths of 0.25, 0.30 and 0.35 mm, and entered the left (ventral) arm of a T-maze first in 128 of 128 trials regardless of where the food was. A 3 × 3 grid maze that requires two right turns was unsolvable in principle. Under the SMD-direction rule (ADR-017), which permits dorsal bends through whitelisted stimulation, retraining on the right-turn corridor succeeded (reach 1.00) but produced a one-handed policy that failed the left-turn corridor (0.00); policies not trained on right turns kept turning left (Fig 5). The rule preserved open-ground chemotaxis (0.871, CI 0.824–0.907). Bend usage in the retrained open-ground policy was 0.77 dorsal to 0.11 ventral, the opposite of real animals (0.24 dorsal [12]); because the model has no dorsal–ventral cost asymmetry, its preferred direction is set by initialisation, and the ventral preference of real worms therefore lies outside what this model can explain.

### At a T-junction temporal sensing cannot choose the arm, so the policy enters left and reverses when wrong

Policies trained in the T-maze with odour and wall contact reached the food in 100 % of trials and finished in the food arm in 100 %, but they never chose the arm at the junction. First entry was into the left arm in 128 of 128 trials under both bend rules, and food on the right cost 7–38 s more than food on the left across policies (for example 22 s versus 40 s). The correction was not a discrete choice but repeated 2 s reversal bouts that carried the worm tail-first back through the junction and into the other arm, a corridor form of klinokinesis. Wall-contact observations were unnecessary: zeroing them at evaluation left reach and arm at 1.00 and shortened the median time from 40 s to 30 s, and a policy trained with odour only performed identically, while a policy trained with contact only converged to stillness (reach 0.00). In this model the "multisensory" requirement of real maze navigation [3] is met by odour plus wall physics, not by wall sensing.

The reason is informational. At the junction both arms present the same concentration to a nose that senses only the temporal history, so which arm is correct can only be found by entering one. Adding proprioception (head curvature) made navigation faster (T-maze median 26.5 s) but did not change first entry (128 of 128 left) and was used instead to time the deep bend to the head-swing phase. The pre-registered prediction that dorsal bends would let the policy choose the arm (H5-6) was therefore rejected: dorsal bends give the repertoire, not the decision.

Why a correctly aimed dorsal bend still failed to enter the right arm was resolved by recording body frames at the junction (E1). The head entered the right arm to x ≈ 0.45 mm and then stalled against the wall; the stall registered as a weak fall in concentration (Δlog C −0.13 to −0.15 per step), which triggered the open-ground reflex, reverse with a ventral head bias, and returned the worm to the left arm. Gating that reflex off during frontal wall contact abolished all cornering (reach 0.00), and masking SMDV or AVA at the junction reduced completion to 0.00 and 0.50. The stall reflex is thus both the means of turning corners and the cause of aborting a right entry.

### Lateral concentration information selects the bend direction

When the observation included the log-concentration difference between two points 0.1 mm to the left and right of the nose, an idealisation of the head-swing sampling of real animals, the policy used it (Fig 6A). On open ground it reached the source in 98.8 % of trials with a median of 16.5 s, the speed of the oracle. It used both bend directions (2,180 dorsal, 3,251 ventral pulses in 128 episodes), and the realised direction of the deep bend matched the side of the source in 0.73 of pulses, against 0.62 for the same rule without lateral information (chance 0.5). Because pulses within an episode are not independent, we recomputed the statistic per episode: 0.73 ± 0.08 (all 128 episodes above 0.5) versus 0.61 ± 0.18 (88 above, 35 below), Mann–Whitney p = 2.6 × 10⁻⁸, Cohen's d 0.85. This reproduces, in the model, the sensory-guided choice of dorsal versus ventral turns reported for real animals [11]. In the T-maze, however, the lateral-information policy still entered the left arm first in 128 of 128 trials, even though at the junction it fired dorsal pulses toward a right-hand goal in 0.80 of cases, because of the stall reflex described above; the maze-trained version abandoned dorsal pulses and reduced the cost of a right-hand goal to 7 s.

### Robustness of the reported numbers

All headline numbers held at the fine integration step required by our reporting rule. On the same 128 starts, integrating the network at 0.05 ms in double precision instead of 0.25 ms in single precision gave reach rates of 0.914 (versus 0.938) for the open-ground policy, 0.875 (versus 0.844) for the ADR-017 policy, and 1.00 with 128 of 128 left first entries in the T-maze under both settings; only maze transit time changed (median 26.5 → 33.5 s). Conclusions were robust to corridor width (0.25–0.35 mm) and to training seed in the sense stated above, and Wilson 95 % confidence intervals for the key comparisons are given in Table 2.

## Discussion

The central result is a partition. Inside the fixed connectome lie the sufficiency of five command and steering interneurons for chemotaxis, the necessity of reversal, deep bend and steering together, and a contribution of the wiring that is carried by gap junctions. Outside it lie the sensory-to-command step, which the fitted network does not perform, and the two ingredients that decide arm choice in a narrow maze, the dorsal–ventral direction of the deep bend and a lateral sample of the gradient. The model does not say that the connectome is unimportant for these; it says that in an atlas-fitted network with a literature motor layer, they must be supplied from elsewhere, and it names what they are.

The learned strategy converged on the pirouette rule of real animals [9] without being told it, and did so under the narrowest channel set (AVB, AVA, RIV) as well as the full one. This convergence was not sufficiency: a run-and-tumble policy with a fixed 180° reorientation cannot navigate, and every successful policy combined the rule with SMD steering that modulates the bend angle. Real pirouettes likewise have a distribution of reorientation angles and are coupled to head steering [13]. The convergence is evidence that the rule is a property of the task and the motor repertoire rather than of a particular circuit, whereas the necessity of a deep bend follows from temporal sensing, as the lateral-information experiment showed: with a left–right sample the worm went straight to the source at the oracle's speed, using the weathervane mechanism [10] in the form of bend direction rather than of continuous steering. Our failure to learn a weathervane from proprioception plus temporal sensing at a 0.5 s decision interval is consistent with the head-swing sampling of real animals being faster than our observations.

The wiring-shuffle controls address a natural objection: if the agent stimulates command neurons directly, perhaps the wiring is irrelevant. It is not. Random wiring with identical weights, counts and signs performed at a fifth of the real wiring after retraining, and the loss was exactly the loss of deep-bend pulses, that is, of the spread of stimulation to RIV and SMD. The layer dissociation, intact with shuffled chemical synapses and collapsed with shuffled gap junctions, has a clear caveat: the atlas fit had already reduced chemical conductances thirty-fold. We therefore claim a property of the fitted dynamics, not of the animal. It is, however, the same property that the atlas reported directly, that gap junctions and extrasynaptic signalling explain much of what anatomy alone does not [2], and it suggests that the fit and the shuffle test are probing the same structure. Whether the chemical layer becomes necessary under a different fit, for example one that separates inhibitory operating points, is a question the pipeline can answer; our own attempt to do so did not improve validation and was recorded as negative.

The maze results make three predictions for real animals. First, in T-mazes with corridors a few body widths wide, the arm entered should be predicted by the dorsal or ventral direction of the omega bend executed at the junction, which can be read from high-speed video. Second, conditions that suppress dorsal turns, whose spontaneous fraction is about 24 % and falls to 16 % during boundary escape [12], should bias animals toward the ventral arm and delay reaching a goal in the other arm; the wild-type absence of side bias (decision index 0.03 [3]) then requires that dorsal bends be used at the junction at a rate that compensates. Third, stalling against a wall should trigger a pirouette, because in the model the same reflex that takes corners aborts a wrongly aimed entry. The model also reinterprets the mechanosensory requirement of maze navigation [3]: wall-contact observations were unnecessary for our agent, so the failure of mec-4 mutants is more likely to reflect the use of wall contact for propulsion [14] or for memory formation than for arm choice, a distinction that can be tested by separating the two.

The model has limits that bound these claims. The motor layer, steering and deep-bend rules are literature-based assumptions outside the connectome, and although each is anchored to a published mechanism [7,11], their parameters were chosen, not fitted. The body has no wall-pushing propulsion [14], so it crawls slowly in corridors, and it has no dorsal–ventral cost asymmetry, so it cannot explain the ventral preference of real turns. The sensory neurons are stimulated as currents, not as transduction of odour; the negative sensory result is a statement about the fitted network, not about real sensory neurons. Odour passes through walls. One learning algorithm and one small policy were used, and a 3 × 3 grid maze had no robust solution, only an open-loop sequence that failed under start jitter. Finally, one of three training seeds converged on a reversal-only local optimum; the sufficiency claim is about the existence of solutions through the wiring, which two seeds and every ablation confirm, not about the reliability of evolution strategies. *[To be updated with E6c.]*

Taken together, the approach treats a connectome as something to be interrogated by an adaptive experimenter rather than as a parameter set to be tuned. Its answers are of two kinds. Positive ones, such as the sufficiency of a command-interneuron interface, identify minimal stimulation targets for behaviour. Negative ones, such as the silence of the sensory step or the impossibility of a right turn with ventral bends, identify where the model, or the animal, must contain something the wiring diagram does not show.

## Methods

*[This section will be reconciled against the methods fact sheet compiled from the code and the decision records; numbers below are those recorded in `docs/DECISIONS.md` ADR-001 to ADR-017 and `docs/PLAN_WORMGYM.md`.]*

### Network model and fitted dynamics

We used the OpenWorm c302 framework [6] at level C2 (302 neurons, graded synapses and gap junctions from the published connectome [4,5]) and re-implemented its equations in JAX so that hundreds of worms can be simulated in parallel on a GPU. The reference network was generated with c302 and parsed from NeuroML; the 3,364 neuron-to-neuron chemical synapses and 1,084 gap junctions were never modified. Six global parameters (log conductance scales of excitatory, inhibitory and gap-junction connections and of the leak, and the half-activation voltage and slope of the graded synapse) were fitted to the signal-propagation atlas of Randi et al. [2] by gradient descent on the correlation between simulated and measured responses to single-neuron stimulation, with a held-out validation split (variant "Vfit", θ = [−3.44, −6.25, 0.13, 0.69, 1.48, 0.77]). An extension with class-wise parameters and separate inhibitory operating points did not improve validation and was recorded as a negative result. Simulations were integrated with a time step of 0.25 ms in single precision for training and 0.05 ms in double precision for the re-check reported in Results.

### Body and motor layer

The body is a two-dimensional viscoelastic rod of 24 rows crawling on agar with resistive-force drag, driven by the bistable motor units of Boyle, Berri and Cohen [7] (ADR-012). Two literature-based readouts couple the network to the body (ADR-013, ADR-016, ADR-017). Forward and backward gates open when the depolarisation of AVB or AVA exceeds 3 mV. Head steering biases the resting curvature of the anterior twelve rows in proportion to the SMDD − SMDV depolarisation difference (gain 0.03). A deep-bend pulse, a travelling ventral curvature offset (width 4 rows, duration 2.5 s, amplitude 0.36), is fired once when the RIV depolarisation crosses 30 mV and is re-armed only after RIV falls below threshold; this produces a 174° reorientation on open ground. Under rule ADR-017 the pulse is dorsal when SMDD − SMDV exceeds 10 mV and ventral otherwise, following the neural sequence of directed turning [11]. Walls are line segments with a repulsive potential ½ k_w (r_w − d)² for rod nodes closer than r_w = 60 µm (k_w = 5 × 10³); wall contact of the four head nodes on the left, right and front is available as an observation in maze tasks. The body has no wall-pushing propulsion.

### Environment, observations and reward

The odour field is a Gaussian of width 2 mm centred on the source; the worm starts at the origin with a random heading and the source is placed at 1.5 or 2.5 mm in a random direction. An episode lasts 40 s (120 s in the T-maze, 150 s in the grid) and ends when the nose comes within 0.5 mm of the source. Every 0.5 s the policy observes the log concentration at the nose and its four most recent changes (scaled ×10), plus, where stated, the three wall-contact values, the mean curvature of the six head joints, or the log-concentration difference between points 0.1 mm to the left and right of the nose. The reward per step is 100 ΔC − 0.05, with +10 on reaching the source and −2 × the final distance in mm; in the dwelling task the episode continues after arrival and each step within the radius earns +0.5, with an optional movement cost. Mazes are lattices of open cells of width 0.3 mm: an L-shaped corridor with one left or right corner, a T-maze with a 3.0 mm stem and 2.1 mm arms with the source at the end of one arm chosen at random, and a 3 × 3 grid.

### Policy, whitelist and training

The policy is a multilayer perceptron with 16 tanh hidden units whose outputs pass through a sigmoid to stimulation amplitudes between 0 and 6 pA for each channel. A channel stimulates both members of a bilateral pair. The command and steering whitelist is AVB, AVA, SMDD, SMDV, RIV, with RIS added in the dwelling task; the sensory whitelist is ASE, AWC, AWA, ASK, ASH, PLM, ALM, AVM. Motor neurons and muscles are never stimulated. Policies were trained with OpenAI evolution strategies [8] (antithetic perturbations, σ = 0.2, learning rate 0.1, rank-normalised fitness), with a population of 32 candidates × 8 episodes for 40 generations at 1.5 mm and then 64 × 4 for 100 generations at 2.5 mm starting from the 1.5 mm policy; maze policies were initialised from the open-ground policy with zero weights on new inputs. Evaluation always used 128–256 fresh starts with fixed seeds. Ablations mask a channel's output at evaluation; sufficiency tests retrain without the channel.

### Controls and statistics

Wiring shuffles permute the postsynaptic neuron of every neuron-to-neuron chemical synapse and one end of every gap junction with a fixed random seed, preserving weights, counts, signs and the identity of stimulated and readout neurons; layer-specific shuffles permute one class only. The oracle controller drives the motor layer directly with forward drive and a head bias proportional to the bearing to the source. Reversal-onset rates were computed as AVA command crossings per second within quintiles of the observed dC/dt. Reach rates are given with Wilson 95 % confidence intervals; the per-episode bend-direction statistic uses a two-sided sign test against 0.5 and a one-sided Mann–Whitney U test between policies. All pre-registered hypotheses, criteria and verdicts are listed in Table 1 and in the repository.

Simulator, training and analysis code, all decision records and logs are at github.com/kairess/worm-whisperer; recorded simulations can be viewed at kairess.github.io/worm-whisperer.
